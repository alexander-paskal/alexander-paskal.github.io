There are many different approaches to learning. There are top-down approaches such as project-based learning, where you start doing something and then progressively learn the components necessary to do the project that you're interested in. There is a bottom-up approach, where you outline the fundamentals of a particular task or skill and learn from first principles, each first in isolation and then in intersection.

However, the most effective way for learning any skill (in my humble opinion) is the apprenticeship. The apprenticeship effectively involves some sort of expert bringing the learner along while executing the task in question. The learner is first tasked with watching and learning, and then bit by bit they transition from observation to execution, taking on progressively more and more responsibility and operating a higher and higher level of autonomy.

Why is this good? Well to answer this, we should first answer the question of "what is the goal when we're learning?" The answer to this can vary widely depending on ~~what~~ we're learning. For example:
- A math PhD might be learning a set of theorems which could form the basis for their intellectual landscape when attempting to solve proofs
- An electrician might be learning the tools, tasks and underlying concepts necessary to complete a task
- A soccer player might be learning how the proper mechanics for kicking a ball, as well as learning defensive strategies and tactics.

What do these have in common? Well, fundamentally, we can think of learning and skill development as two essential components:
1. the elaboration of a taxonomy
2. the weighting of each individual element

Let's make this concrete with an example - take the soccer player. Say he takes the project-based approach and just starts playing a lot. He will encounter a very minimal set of variables to consider - only what he himself notices. Through play, if his mind is engaged he will learn to weight these elements in accordance with a given state of play, such that he knows when to blast the ball, when the tap it, when to pass it, etc. But in order for him to truly learn the richness of the game, it would take him years of study and organic insights. This is a very slow exploration space.

Now let's say that he, instead of just playing, just reads about the game. He learns theories, studies mechanics, and learns all of the nuances that there is to know about the game. He has an extremely diverse set. However, he hasn't learned to properly weight these elements - maybe only a very small number actually matter in many given instances.

The third approach combines the two - the player learns the taxonomy implicitly through imitation of and explicitly through instruction from the expert. The player also learns to weight the elements of the craft by acquiring experience. 


This is the ideal way to go from novice to expert.
